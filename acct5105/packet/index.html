1. Course overview

ACCT 5105 is financial reporting and assurance in a blockchain. You leave able to read a shared ledger the way you read a set of books: what was recorded, what it is worth, and what an assurer can stand behind.

Bulletin description (required). Blockchain Technology has been described as the most important development in financial reporting since the emergence of double-entry bookkeeping. This course is designed to familiarize participants with Blockchain technology, and to explore the implications of Blockchain on the preparation and reliability of financial statements, as well as on the assurance process of financial statements. Many topics covered in this course may also be applicable to industries in which record keeping plays a prominent role (for example real estate transactions, recording health care information). This course is required for MS Analytics–Accounting students. Credit 1.5 units.

The course sits at the intersection of financial reporting, assurance, and blockchain. Distributed ledgers change how finance keeps the books. You will learn the architecture that matters for that work: decentralization, cryptographic security, immutability, and transparency, and how those features change the preparation, verification, and audit of financial statements. You will also see what blockchain does to internal controls, audit procedures, and regulatory compliance.

The hands-on case is a class testnet coin, Eagecoin (ticker EAGE), priced against instructor-issued CLASSUSD. You record it, trace it, and assure it the way you would a digital-asset balance — not as a product pitch. Testnet only. No mainnet and no real money. CLASSUSD and EAGE are play tokens for this classroom. They are not money, not a security offering, and not investment advice.

2. Learning objectives

By the end of the course you can:

  1. Blockchain fundamentals. Explain a distributed ledger, a consensus mechanism, and a block structure, including how a transaction is recorded and linked so it cannot be quietly rewritten.
  2. Financial reporting. Say where blockchain can improve accuracy, transparency, and real-time validation of financial data, and where it cuts reconciliation — and where it does not.
  3. Audit and assurance. Use the chain for verification, fraud signals, and risk assessment, and say which traditional audit procedures have to change.
  4. Regulation and ethics. Apply accounting standards, legal frameworks, and the auditor’s ethical duties when the records are on a chain.
  5. Practice. Complete case work and notebook exercises that connect a blockchain record to an accounting information system: issue, circulate, measure, and then report what an assurer would rely on.

How we make the ideas stick. Never studied finance or blockchain? Open Start here first. Every session notebook opens with Words we use today, runs Demo cells you execute, then YOUR TURN, and closes with Check you understand. The longer explanation lives in the concept handbook. Use it if you missed class or a word still will not land (about 10 minutes).

3. Who should take this

Program. ACCT 5105-01 is a required course in Washington University’s Olin Business School Master of Science in Business Analytics, Accounting Analytics concentration. You bring accounting judgment together with SQL, Python, R, and data analytics.

Who it is for. You will advise on digital-asset accounting and blockchain-based transactions, read what a shared ledger does to reporting and compliance, and say what an assurance procedure can and cannot verify.

Where it leads. Public accounting, corporate finance, and consulting in a digital-first environment.

Prerequisites. Comfort with financial statements and with a Python notebook. SQL or R is useful and not assumed in every lab. No prior Solidity. No prior crypto required.

Out of scope. Writing production smart contracts from scratch, mining hardware, token-price forecasting, mainnet, or treating every database problem as a blockchain problem.

9. Late work, missed quizzes, and regrades

Late gates and labs (A1–A6, L1–L3). Canvas time is official. 10 percentage points off per 24 hours, starting at the due stamp. After 48 hours the score is zero unless §19 Disability Resources, a religious holiday notice, or a written instructor exception (illness, hospitalization, or a documented emergency) applies. “My laptop died at 9:14” is not an exception if the work was not started. Email before the deadline if you see a problem.

A7. No late memo. If it is not in Canvas at 6:15 on Dec 14, the memo score is zero. You may still do A8.

A8. If you miss demo day without a prior written exception, the demo score is zero. A team can present if at least one listed teammate is in the room with the proofs; absent teammates share the team score unless the instructor was told in writing that they left the team.

Missed in-room quiz (Q1–Q3). No drop-the-lowest. Makeup only for (1) a Disability Resources letter that covers tests, (2) a religious holiday notice under §19, or (3) a written instructor exception before the quiz when possible (illness that night: email before 6:15, or as soon as you can). Habif does not issue routine “excuse notes”; do not wait for one. A makeup is a different sitting, still closed notes, scheduled once.

Q4. Canvas timestamp. The late rule for gates applies.

Regrade. Email the instructor within 7 days of the score appearing in Canvas, with the question number and one paragraph on the error. The whole artifact may be re-read. Scores can go down. After 7 days the score stands.

Partial credit (how we mark). Arithmetic: 50% if the method is right and one number is wrong. Short answers: mechanism, class application, caveat. “Because decentralization” with no mechanism is 0.

12. Generative AI

WashU requires a written GenAI rule. In this course:

You may use a chatbot to (1) explain a word after you have read Start here, (2) debug a Python error message, or (3) check grammar on a sentence you already wrote.

You may not use a chatbot to write A1–A7, invent transaction hashes, fill YOUR TURN for you, or take a quiz. If a sentence is in your PDF, you must be able to explain it out loud in office hours without the model.

If you used a tool for allowed help, add one line at the end: “AI used for: grammar / debug / word meaning.” Hiding it is an integrity issue. Pasting a generated unbundle paragraph is an integrity issue even with a citation.

13. Integrity and conduct (this course)

  • Labs: discussion allowed; the written PDF and the notebook answers must be your own.
  • Quizzes: individual, closed notes.
  • Gates A1–A6: teammates may talk; one PDF; do not copy another team’s community or hashes.
  • A seed phrase in a submission is a security fail (points off and a private reset), not a clever appendix. If the seed was posted publicly, it is also an integrity case.
  • Mainnet deploy or a public sale for this course is a zero on the project (the 40% final, and the related gate if the sale used class work).
  • Do not record the class, the quizzes, or a classmate’s seed or address list for posting online.
  • CLASSUSD faucet: one drip per roster address. Do not script the faucet.

University integrity language is in §19. The instructor still assigns the grade on the work.
